A model that fits its training data perfectly can still be useless. It
may have learned the noise in those particular points rather than the
pattern behind them, and the only way to find out is to test it on data
it never saw. That's why several pages on this site hold back a test set.
This page shows what goes wrong without one.

## The dataset

`data.csv` holds 36 drives of one car, each at a steady `speed` in km/h,
with its fuel use in `litres` per 100 km. The data is synthetic, and we
know the truth: fuel use follows a gentle U, lowest near 65 km/h, plus
noise. The `split` column marks 12 drives as `train` and 24 as `test`.
We kept the training set small on purpose, because that's when
overfitting is easiest to see.

## The method

We fit a polynomial of each degree from 1 to 8 to the training drives
alone. A degree-$d$ polynomial has $d + 1$ coefficients:

$$
\hat{y} = c_0 + c_1 z + c_2 z^2 + \cdots + c_d z^d
$$

Here $z$ is the speed mapped onto −1 to 1, using the training range of 36
to 116 km/h. Without that step, degree 8 would add up raw speeds to the
16th power, around $10^{33}$, next to small numbers, and the arithmetic
would lose accuracy.

**Least squares** picks the coefficients that make the squared errors on
the training drives as small as possible. Setting the slope of that error
to zero gives $d + 1$ linear equations in $d + 1$ unknowns, the **normal
equations**, and each implementation solves them by **Gaussian
elimination**: subtract multiples of each row from the rows below until
the system is triangular, then solve from the bottom up.

Then we measure each fit by its root-mean-square error, in litres per
100 km, once on the training drives and once on the test drives:

$$
\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (\hat{y}_i - y_i)^2}
$$

## Reading the results

| Degree | Train RMSE | Test RMSE |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 0.529 | 0.779 |
| 2 | 0.284 | 0.391 |
| 3 | 0.278 | 0.405 |
| 4 | 0.272 | 0.390 |
| 5 | 0.220 | 0.546 |
| 6 | 0.186 | 0.630 |
| 7 | 0.149 | 0.549 |
| 8 | 0.074 | 1.153 |

The training error falls at every step. Each extra coefficient gives the
curve one more way to bend toward the 12 points, so it can only fit them
better. By degree 8, it nearly runs through every one.

The test error tells the real story. A straight line is too stiff to
follow the U. Degree 2, the true shape, cuts the test error in half.
Degrees 3 and 4 barely change it. From degree 5, the test error rises,
reaching 1.153 at degree 8, the worst of all.

Look at where the training speeds fall: five between 36 and 54 km/h,
five between 101 and 116, and only two, 71 and 81, in between. In that
gap, the degree-8 curve is free to wiggle. At 70 km/h it predicts 3.90
litres, and at 90 it predicts 6.51, when the truth is 4.52 and 5.06.
Four test drives also sit just outside the training range, where every
polynomial has to extrapolate. But even the 20 test drives inside the
range give degree 8 an error of 0.80, against 0.33 for degree 2.

Degrees 2 and 4 nearly tie on the test set. When they do, choose the
simpler one: it has fewer ways to be wrong on data you haven't seen yet.

## What to watch for across languages

None of these implementations calls a fitter or a solver, though several
languages ship one: Octave's `polyfit` and backslash, Julia's `\`, APL's
`⌹`, Ada's `Solve`, and R's `lm()`. NumPy's `polyfit` would do it in
Python, but NumPy isn't installed. Each implementation writes out the
elimination instead, and three things vary.

The first is scaling. In Julia, solving the normal equations on raw
speeds with the built-in `\` gives a degree-8 training error of 0.073996
instead of 0.073821, a difference big enough to fail this site's tests.
R's `lm()` gets the right answer even from raw speeds, because it avoids
the normal equations and uses a more stable method, a QR decomposition.

The second is how a row swap works. Where a matrix is a list of rows, as
in Python, Go, Java, Lua, and OCaml, swapping trades two references and
copies no numbers. C can't assign one array row to another, so it swaps
entry by entry, as do Common Lisp and Pascal with their 2-D arrays.
Octave, Julia, R, and APL swap two rows in one assignment by indexing
both at once. In Swift, Tcl, and PHP, arrays are values, so `solve`
works on its own copy and the caller's equations stay untouched.

The third is the small traps along the way. APL reads `K-1↓⍳N` as
`K-(1↓⍳N)`, since it evaluates right to left. In PostgreSQL, `m[2]` on a
2-D array gives NULL, not row 2; you need `m[2:2]`. SWI-Prolog's `/`
returns an integer when it divides exactly, so the code converts each
scaled speed to a float. And COBOL works in fixed-point decimals, with 18
digits after the point, and still matches double precision to within
1e-11.
