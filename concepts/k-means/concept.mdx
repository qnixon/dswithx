k-means groups points into $k$ clusters. Each cluster has a centroid, and
the algorithm alternates between assigning points to their nearest centroid
and moving each centroid to the mean of its points, until nothing changes.
Unlike k-nearest neighbors, it has no labeled examples to learn from. You
hand it raw points and a number $k$, and it finds the groups on its own.

## The dataset

`data.csv` holds 150 synthetic points, `x` and `y`, generated once by
`make_data.py` (fixed seed 20261101) and committed. It draws three round
blobs of 50 points, centered at (2, 2), (6, 3), and (3.5, 6), with a
standard deviation of 1.3. The centers are only 3.9 to 4.3 apart, so the
blobs overlap.

The first three rows come one from each blob, because k-means here starts
from them. The generator also rejects any draw where a point is ever within
$10^{-6}$ of being equally close to two centroids, since rounding could then
send it different ways in different languages. The first draw passed.

## The method

With $k = 3$:

1. Start with the first three points as the centroids.
2. **Assign** every point to its nearest centroid by Euclidean distance.
3. If no point changed cluster since the last assignment, stop.
4. **Update** each centroid to the mean of its assigned points, then go back
   to step 2.

`iterations` counts the assignment steps, including the final one where
nothing changed. `inertia` is the sum of squared distances from each point to
its centroid:

$$
\text{inertia} = \sum_{i} \lVert p_i - c_{a(i)} \rVert^2
$$

Why does it stop? Both steps can only lower the inertia. Moving a point to
its nearest centroid can't increase its squared distance, and the mean is
the position with the smallest total squared distance to a group. There are
finitely many ways to split the points, so the falling inertia must run out.

On this data:

- **Pass 1** assigns all 150 points: 45, 51, and 54 per cluster. The update
  moves the centroids from (1.25, 1.66), (6.42, 3.18), and (2.41, 5.11) to
  (1.555, 1.913), (6.203, 3.113), and (3.011, 5.454). Inertia falls from
  467.03 to 431.41.
- **Pass 2** moves 4 points, and no centroid then moves more than 0.16.
- **Passes 3 and 4** each move 2 points from cluster 3 to cluster 1.
  Cluster 2 no longer changes, so its centroid stays put.
- **Pass 5** changes nothing, and the loop stops.

The first step does most of the work. After that, only points near the
borders switch sides, and the centroids drift by hundredths.

## Reading the results

The final centroids are (1.700, 2.138), (6.205, 3.043), and (3.131, 5.750),
holding 52, 50, and 48 points, with an inertia of 423.63. Each lies 0.21 to
0.45 from one of the true centers.

Two things cause the gap. Each blob is only a sample of 50 points, so its
own mean isn't exactly its center. And the blobs overlap: 15 of the 150
points end up in a cluster other than the blob they came from. k-means
can't know where a point came from. It only minimizes inertia, and by that
measure it beats the truth: grouping points by their real blobs gives an
inertia of 487.47.

The answer also depends on the starting centroids, and the algorithm never
chooses $k$ for you. We fixed both, so every language has one result to
match.

## What to watch for across languages

None of the from-scratch implementations calls a ready-made k-means. R has
one, `kmeans()`, and the R tab's **Built-in** switch shows it. Its defaults
aren't this page's method, though: it starts from random points and uses
the Hartigan-Wong algorithm, which moves single points between clusters.
Two runs with different seeds found inertias of 422.90 and 422.17, both a
little lower than our 423.63. Passing the first three points and
`algorithm = "Lloyd"` reproduces our result exactly.

The from-scratch versions use general tools: a distance formula (Python's
`math.dist`, Go's `math.Hypot`, Fortran's `norm2`, written out by hand
elsewhere), sums, counts, and picking the smallest value. Watch how each
holds 2-D points: tuples, nested arrays, a matrix, structs in Go, C, C++, and Swift, records in Java, a `defstruct` in Common Lisp, an `X-Y` term in Prolog,
fixed-size arrays in Rust, columns of a 2-D array in Fortran, separate x
and y arrays in awk and PostgreSQL, or a table of records in COBOL. Watch
how each notices that nothing changed: seventeen compare the whole assignment
with the last one, while awk, COBOL, and C set a flag as they go. And
watch the three languages with no loops: Haskell's and Prolog's `run`
call themselves with the moved centroids, and PostgreSQL repeats the two steps with
`WITH RECURSIVE`.
