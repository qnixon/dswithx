Every model on this site has settings you choose before it learns anything:
$k$ in k-nearest neighbors, the depth of a decision tree, the learning rate in
logistic regression. Cross-validation is how you choose them honestly. It
holds back part of the training data, tests the model there, and repeats
until every part has had its turn.

## The dataset

`data.csv` is the k-nearest neighbors data, unchanged: 200 synthetic wheat
seeds, kama or rosa, with kernel `length_mm` and `width_mm`. The `split`
column marks 150 as `train` and 50 as `test`. The kNN page fixed $k = 5$ and
got 47 of the 50 test seeds right. Here we ask which $k$ we should have used.

## Why not just use the test seeds?

Try every $k$ on the test seeds and you'd pick $k = 3$, which gets 48 of
50 right. But the test seeds exist to estimate how the model does on seeds
it has never seen. Once they've chosen $k$, they've been seen, and 48 of 50
is flattering, not honest.

## The method

1. **Split** the 150 training seeds into 5 folds of 30. The rows are
   already shuffled, so fold 1 is rows 1 to 30, fold 2 rows 31 to 60, and
   so on.
2. **For each odd** $k$ from 1 to 15 (odd, so a two-way vote can't tie),
   hold out each fold in turn, classify its 30 seeds using the other 120, and count the correct
   ones. Every training seed is held out exactly once, so the
   cross-validated accuracy is
   $$
   \text{CV}(k) = \frac{\text{correct across all 5 folds}}{150}
   $$
3. **Pick** the $k$ with the highest CV accuracy. On a tie, the smaller $k$
   wins.
4. **Test once.** Classify the 50 test seeds using all 150 training seeds
   and the chosen $k$, and report that score.

## Reading the results

| $k$ | Correct per fold | CV accuracy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 27, 28, 27, 27, 25 | 0.893 |
| 3 | 28, 29, 28, 27, 26 | 0.920 |
| 5 | 28, 29, 29, 28, 27 | 0.940 |
| 7 | 28, 29, 29, 28, 28 | 0.947 |
| 9 | 28, 29, 27, 28, 28 | 0.933 |
| 11 | 28, 28, 28, 28, 27 | 0.927 |
| 13 | 28, 28, 27, 28, 27 | 0.920 |
| 15 | 28, 28, 28, 28, 27 | 0.927 |

Cross-validation picks $k = 7$, at 142 of 150, and it gets 47 of the 50
test seeds right: the same 0.94 as the kNN page's $k = 5$, but now a number
we can trust.

Read across a row and you'll see why one held-out set isn't enough. With
$k = 1$, the folds score anywhere from 25 to 28 out of 30. And if you'd
used a single fold to choose, you'd have picked $k = 3$ with fold 1 or 2,
$k = 5$ with fold 3 or 4, and $k = 7$ only with fold 5. Averaging over all
five folds smooths out that luck.

The extremes lose for opposite reasons: with $k = 1$, one oddly placed
seed misleads its neighbors, and with large $k$, the vote reaches into the
other variety's territory.

## What to watch for across languages

None of these implementations calls a ready-made cross-validation routine;
scikit-learn, R's caret, and Julia's MLJ have them, but aren't installed.
Each language reuses its kNN classifier, so the interest is in how it
carves out a fold.

Watch what the second number in a slice means. Python's `train[30:60]`
stops before row 60, Tcl's `lrange` and Julia's `31:60` include both ends,
and PHP's `array_slice` takes a length instead of an end. Mix them up and a
fold comes out one row short or one row long.

Watch for views, too. Go's `train[a:b]`, Java's `subList`, and C++'s
`std::span` look at the original rows without copying them. That's cheap,
but in Go the obvious
`append(train[:a], train[b:]...)` writes the later folds over the fold
being tested, and cross-validation reports 100% accuracy for $k = 1$. Go
copies with `slices.Concat` instead.

Some languages never cut at all. R drops the fold with a negative index,
`train[-rows, ]`. Octave and APL keep rows with a true-or-false mask. awk,
BASIC, and COBOL skip the held-out rows inside the neighbor search, and
PostgreSQL gives every row a fold number and joins on `fold <> held.fold`.

And watch the tie rule for the best $k$. Haskell's `maximumBy` returns the
last of equal values, so most languages compare with a strict `>`, which
keeps the smaller $k$. APL's `⍳` and PostgreSQL's
`ORDER BY accuracy DESC, k` get the same answer by taking the first.
