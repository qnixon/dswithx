Linear regression fits a straight line through a cloud of points, so you can
describe how one quantity changes with another and predict one from the
other. It is the simplest model that learns from data, and it is built
from the pieces of descriptive statistics: means, squared deviations, and
sums.

## The dataset

`data.csv` holds 100 students' weekly study `hours` and exam `score`. The
data is synthetic. `make_data.py` drew each student's hours uniformly
between 0.5 and 10, then set the score to $42 + 5.2 \times \text{hours}$
plus normal noise with a standard deviation of 6, using the fixed seed
20261030. Scores were kept between 0 and 100, and 3 students hit the cap at
exactly 100. We ran it once and committed the result.

Because we made the data, you can check how close least squares gets to
the true line.

## The idea: least squares

Any line $\hat{y} = a + bx$ makes a prediction for each student. The gap
between a student's real score and the line's prediction is a **residual**.
Least squares picks the intercept $a$ and slope $b$ that make the sum of
the squared residuals as small as possible. As with variance, squaring
stops misses above and below the line from canceling out, and it punishes
big misses more than small ones.

That minimization has a closed-form answer. With means $\bar{x}$ and
$\bar{y}$, define the sums of squares and cross-products

$$
S_{xx} = \sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2 \qquad
S_{yy} = \sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2 \qquad
S_{xy} = \sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})
$$

Then the least-squares slope and intercept, and the correlation $r$, are

$$
b = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}} \qquad a = \bar{y} - b\,\bar{x} \qquad
r = \frac{S_{xy}}{\sqrt{S_{xx}\,S_{yy}}}
$$

Here is the intuition. $S_{xy}$ asks whether students who study more than
average also score more than average. Each term is positive when both
deviations have the same sign and negative when they differ. In this data,
93 of the 100 terms are positive, and $S_{xy}$ comes to 4720.63. Dividing by
$S_{xx} = 907.30$, the spread in hours, turns that into points per hour:
$b = 5.20$. The intercept then follows from one fact: the fitted line always
passes through the point of means, here 5.027 hours and 68.381 points.

The correlation $r$ uses the same $S_{xy}$, but scales it by the spread in
both variables, so it has no units and always lies between −1 and 1.

## Reading the results

The fitted slope is 5.202961: each extra hour of study goes with about 5.2
more points. The intercept is 42.225716, the predicted score for a student
who studies zero hours. Read it with care, since nobody in the data studied
less than half an hour. Both land very close to the 5.2 and 42 we used to
generate the data.

The correlation is $r = 0.927659$, a strong positive relationship. Its
square, $r^2 = 0.860551$, is the share of the variation in scores that the
line explains. The squared deviations of the scores from their mean add up
to $S_{yy} = 28541.33$. The squared residuals around the line add up to
only 3980.07, and $1 - 3980.07 / 28541.33$ is exactly $r^2$. The other 14%
is the noise. The root-mean-square residual is 6.31 points, close to the
standard deviation of 6 we put in.

## What to watch for across languages

None of the from-scratch implementations calls a ready-made regression or
correlation function, even where the language has one: no `lm()` or `cor()`
in R, no `statistics.linear_regression` in Python, no `regr_slope` or
`corr` in PostgreSQL, no backslash solve, `X \ y`, in Julia or Octave,
and no `polyfit` in Octave. The R, Python,
and PostgreSQL tabs have a **Built-in** switch that shows those calls, and
they print the same results. The from-scratch versions don't call a
built-in mean either. They use everyday building blocks: arithmetic, a
square root, and in most languages a built-in `sum` (PostgreSQL adds
`count`, C, BASIC, and Lua, which have no `sum`, write every total as a loop, OCaml, with no `sum` either, writes one as a fold, and C++ uses
`std::accumulate` and `std::inner_product`).

Watch for how each language makes the two passes the formulas need. The
means come first, then the deviations from them. Watch how it pairs each
`hours` value with its `score`: Haskell, for one, reads two lists and
`zip`s them back together, as Swift does, and Common Lisp's `mapcar`, Prolog's
`maplist`, OCaml's `List.map2`, and Tcl's two-list `foreach` walk two lists side by side. And watch how much code goes into reading
two columns by name compared with the regression itself.
