k-nearest neighbors (kNN) classifies a new point by finding the $k$
labelled points closest to it and taking a majority vote. The assumption
is that things that measure alike are probably the same kind. There is no
training step: kNN keeps every training point and does its work when you ask
about a new one. The data is the model.

## The dataset

`data.csv` holds 200 synthetic wheat seeds, 100 each of two varieties, `kama`
and `rosa`, with kernel `length_mm` and `width_mm`. We drew it once with
`make_data.py` (fixed seed 20261031) from normal distributions: kama seeds
centered at 5.5 mm by 3.2 mm, rosa at 6.2 mm by 3.6 mm. The varieties overlap
a little, so some seeds are genuinely hard to call. The rows are shuffled,
and the `split` column marks 150 as `train` and 50 as `test`.

The generator rejects any draw where a test seed's 5th and 6th nearest
training seeds are less than 10⁻⁶ mm apart in distance; it took 32 draws to
pass. Otherwise, which seed got 5th place would depend on each language's
tie-breaking or floating-point rounding, and the answers could differ.

## The method

For each test seed, with $k = 5$:

1. Compute the Euclidean distance to every training seed:
   $d = \sqrt{(\ell_1 - \ell_2)^2 + (w_1 - w_2)^2}$.
2. Take the 5 nearest training seeds.
3. Predict the variety most of them have. With two varieties and an odd $k$,
   the vote cannot tie.

Deep inside a cluster, every neighbor agrees: 40 of the 50 test seeds got a
5–0 vote. Near the border, neighbors mix. Take the test seed at 5.96 mm by
3.21 mm, a kama:

| Neighbor (length × width) | Variety | Distance (mm) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5.99 × 3.18 | rosa | 0.042 |
| 5.94 × 3.27 | kama | 0.063 |
| 5.88 × 3.14 | kama | 0.106 |
| 5.90 × 3.12 | kama | 0.108 |
| 5.93 × 3.35 | rosa | 0.143 |

Kama wins 3 to 2, correctly, even though the single nearest seed is a rosa.
With $k = 1$ this seed would be misclassified. A larger $k$ outvotes one
unlucky neighbor; too large a $k$ pulls in seeds from far away.

## Reading the results

47 of the 50 test seeds are classified correctly, an accuracy of 0.94. The
three misses were all 3–2 votes in the overlap. A rosa at 5.84 × 3.32 is
closer to the kama center than to its own, a kama at 6.06 × 3.21 is closer
to the rosa center, and a kama at 5.87 × 3.36 is nearly equidistant (0.403
mm versus 0.408 mm). Any method that sees only length and width would
struggle with them.

Distance adds up differences across features, so a feature measured in
bigger numbers counts for more. Here both are millimetres with similar
spreads: standard deviations of 0.46 mm for length and 0.27 mm for width. Record width in
micrometres and length stops counting: accuracy drops to 45 of 50, the same
as using width alone. The fix is feature scaling, such as rescaling each
feature to a standard deviation of 1.

## What to watch for across languages

None of these implementations calls a ready-made kNN classifier. They use
general tools: arithmetic, square roots, Python's `math.dist`, Fortran's
`norm2`, sorting, and counting helpers such as `Counter`, `tally`,
`table()`, and SQL's `count`. Go, Rust, JavaScript, and Julia have no
counter type, so they count votes in a map or a short list, C compares
labels with `strcmp` and counts them in a loop, Fortran counts matching
labels with `count`, and APL compares every neighbor with every label at
once. awk and COBOL skip the square root and compare squared distances.

Watch how each finds the 5 nearest. Ten of the thirteen sort all 150
distances for every test seed; awk, COBOL, and Fortran pick the nearest
unused seed five times over. Fortran's `minloc` returns the first of equal
distances, which keeps the same order a stable sort would.

Watch tie-breaking too. On an exactly equal distance, all thirteen keep the
earlier training row. Python's `sorted`, R's `order()`, Rust's `sort_by`,
JavaScript's and Julia's `sort`, and APL's `⍋` are stable. Go uses `slices.SortStableFunc`, because its
plain `SortFunc` isn't guaranteed to be. Ruby's `sort_by` and C's `qsort`
aren't stable, so Ruby adds the file position to its sort key, and C's
comparison function breaks ties by row number. But
floating point decides what counts as equal. The test seed at 6.06 × 3.21
has two training seeds exactly 0.13 mm away. COBOL's decimal arithmetic sees
a tie; Python sees 0.1299999999999999 and 0.13000000000000037, so the two
come out in opposite orders. Both are among the five either way, which is
why the generator only guards the 5th/6th boundary.
