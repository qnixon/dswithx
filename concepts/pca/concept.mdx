Principal component analysis (PCA) finds the directions in which your data
varies most. When several columns move together, a few of these directions,
the **principal components**, can stand in for all of them. PCA is used to
compress data, to plot many columns in two dimensions, and to find the
hidden factors behind a set of measurements.

## The dataset

`data.csv` holds 100 synthetic students' scores, from 0 to 100, in `math`,
`physics`, `reading`, and `writing`. We drew it once with `make_data.py`
(fixed seed 20261103) from two hidden traits: a general ability that lifts
all four scores, and a lean that raises math and physics while lowering
reading and writing, or the reverse. Then we added noise. PCA never sees
those traits; it has only the scores.

## The method

1. **Standardize** each column to mean 0 and (sample) standard deviation 1,
   so that no subject counts more just because its scores spread wider.
2. **Build the correlation matrix**, a 4×4 table of how strongly each pair
   of subjects moves together. Math and physics correlate at 0.824, and
   reading and writing at 0.767, but math and reading only at 0.278.
3. **Find its top eigenvector** by power iteration. Start with
   $v = (1, 0, 0, 0)$, then repeat $v \leftarrow Mv / \lVert Mv \rVert$.
   Each multiplication stretches $v$ most along the direction the matrix
   stretches most, so $v$ turns toward it. The **eigenvalue**, how much
   variance lies along that direction, is $\lambda = v^\top M v$.
4. **Deflate**: subtract $\lambda v v^\top$ from the matrix, which removes
   the first component, then repeat step 3 for the second.
5. **Fix the sign.** If $v$ is an eigenvector, so is $-v$, so we agree to
   make each one's first entry positive. Starting from $(1, 0, 0, 0)$
   already lands on that version, but every implementation checks anyway,
   so the rule doesn't depend on the starting point.

We run 1,000 steps, which is generous. After one step, $v$ is
$(0.724, 0.597, 0.202, 0.280)$, still leaning toward where it started.
After 5 steps it's $(0.513, 0.526, 0.447, 0.511)$, and from step 18 on, all
six decimals stay fixed. The second component settles even faster, in 9
steps, because its eigenvalue stands further above the next one.

## Reading the results

| | Component 1 | Component 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Eigenvalue | 2.496154 | 1.110197 |
| Share of variance | 0.624 | 0.278 |
| math | 0.504 | 0.498 |
| physics | 0.518 | 0.469 |
| reading | 0.457 | −0.585 |
| writing | 0.518 | −0.437 |

The four standardized columns have a total variance of 4, so the first
component carries $2.496 / 4 = 62.4\%$ of it, and the two together carry
90.2%. Two numbers per student keep nine-tenths of what four did.

The components are the hidden traits. The first weights every subject
about equally: that's general ability, and the true direction was 0.5 for
each. The second gives math and physics positive weights and reading and
writing negative ones: that's the lean, and the true direction was
$(0.543, 0.453, -0.543, -0.453)$.

Take the first student, who scored 82, 91, 74, and 72. Standardized, that's
$(1.079, 1.800, 0.337, 0.160)$. Multiplying by each component gives
1.712 on the first, well above average overall, and 1.114 on the second,
leaning toward math and physics.

## What to watch for across languages

None of the from-scratch implementations calls a ready-made PCA or
eigenvalue routine. R and Julia have them, and their tabs have a
**Built-in** switch that shows R's `prcomp()` and Julia's `eigen`. Both find
the same eigenvalues as power iteration, but R returns the first component
with every sign flipped, and Julia flips both. That's why the sign rule
matters: with it, the built-ins print the same results. Octave's `eig`
would match too, once its eigenvalues, listed smallest first, are sorted. Ruby's
`Matrix#eigensystem` would work too, but `matrix` is a bundled gem rather
than part of the standard library, so it's left out.

Watch how each holds a matrix. R, Julia, Octave, Fortran, and APL have
matrices built in, so a power-iteration step is one product: `%*%`, `*` in
Julia and Octave, `matmul`, or `+.×`. APL also needs no loop, since `⍣` repeats the step 1,000 times,
and neither does Haskell, whose `iterate` builds the endless list of steps.
Python, Ruby, Java, JavaScript, Swift, Haskell, Common Lisp, and Prolog use arrays or lists of rows with
small helper functions, and Go, Rust, C, C++, and Pascal use fixed-size arrays whose length is part of the type. BASIC uses 2-D arrays sized by `DIM`, with a loop for every product. COBOL nests one
table inside another, awk keys a single array by `m[i, j]`, and PostgreSQL
stores one row per entry, so a matrix-vector product becomes a `GROUP BY`.

And watch COBOL's precision. It works in fixed-point decimals, and its
results match the floating-point languages to 14 decimal places. Power
iteration corrects its own rounding at every step, so even 9 decimal places
would pass.
