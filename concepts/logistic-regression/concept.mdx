Logistic regression predicts a yes-or-no outcome. Instead of fitting a
straight line through the answers, it fits a probability between 0 and 1,
and calls "yes" when that probability is at least 0.5. It's the standard
first model for classification, and a neural network answering a yes-or-no
question ends with the same calculation.

## The dataset

`data.csv` holds 200 synthetic students with weekly study `hours`, nightly
`sleep` hours, and whether they `passed` the exam (1) or not (0). We drew it
once with `make_data.py` (fixed seed 20261101): each student passed with
probability $1 / (1 + e^{-z})$, where $z = -6 + 0.6 \times \text{hours} +
0.5 \times \text{sleep}$. The `split` column marks 150 rows as `train` and 50
as `test`. 92 of the 150 training students passed.

Why not use linear regression? Fit a line to the 0s and 1s here and it
predicts a 1.11 "probability" for one student and −0.07 for another: 23 of
the 150 land outside 0 to 1.

## The model

Logistic regression computes a score $z$ from the features, then squeezes
it into a probability with the **sigmoid** function:

$$
z = w_0 + w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2
\qquad
p = \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}
$$

A score of 0 gives exactly 0.5. Large positive scores approach 1 and large
negative ones approach 0, but never reach them.

To judge a set of weights, we use the **log-loss**: the average of
$-\log p$ for students who passed and $-\log(1 - p)$ for those who didn't.
Confident wrong answers cost a lot; confident right ones cost almost
nothing. Unlike least squares, there is no formula that solves for the best
weights directly, so we search for them.

## The method

1. **Scale** each feature to mean 0 and standard deviation 1, using the
   training rows' mean and sample standard deviation. Study hours (sd 2.71)
   and sleep (sd 1.40) then move on the same scale, so one learning rate
   suits both.
2. **Start** with every weight at 0. Every student gets $p = 0.5$, and the
   log-loss is $\log 2 = 0.693147$.
3. **Step** downhill 1,000 times. The gradient of the log-loss for weight
   $j$ is the average of $(p_i - y_i)\,x_{ij}$ over the training rows, and
   each step subtracts 0.5 times it from every weight at once.

The first step already moves the hours weight furthest, to 0.156, because
the students who passed mostly studied more than average. The loss falls to
0.641 after one step, 0.471 after 10, and 0.419409 after 100; by step 200 it
has settled at 0.419364. Newton's method, which homes in on the best weights
in a few large steps, lands on the same six decimals, so 1,000 steps is plenty.

## Reading the results

The fitted weights are an intercept of 0.753594, 1.924812 for hours, and
0.367271 for sleep. On the scaled features, one standard deviation more
study multiplies the odds of passing by $e^{1.92} = 6.85$; one more of sleep
multiplies them by 1.44.

Take a test student who studied 3.7 hours and slept 6.4. Scaled, that's
−0.7284 for hours and 0.0109 for sleep, so
$z = 0.7536 + 1.9248 \times (-0.7284) + 0.3673 \times 0.0109 = -0.644$ and
$p = 0.344$. The model says "fail", and the student did fail.

The model gets 37 of the 50 test students right, an accuracy of 0.74.
Predicting "pass" for everyone would score 0.68, since 34 of the 50 passed.
Nine of the 13 misses are students who passed despite a low predicted
probability. Converted back to hours, the fit finds 0.71 per study hour and
0.26 per hour of sleep, against the 0.6 and 0.5 we generated with: 150
students is not much data, and sleep's smaller effect is the harder one to
pin down.

## What to watch for across languages

None of these implementations calls a ready-made logistic regression. Of the
twenty-seven languages, only R ships one, `glm(..., family = binomial)`, which finds
the same weights to six decimals in five steps of a cleverer method. The
rest use general tools: `exp`, `log`, square roots, sums, and loops.

Watch how each takes one step. R, Julia, Octave, Fortran, and APL do it
with matrix products (`%*%`, `*` in Julia and Octave, `matmul`, and `+.×`),
with no loop over students. In Octave, the dot in the sigmoid's `./` matters:
with a plain `/`, the first step quietly builds a 150 by 150 matrix. Python, Ruby, JavaScript, Swift, Haskell, OCaml, Elixir, Common Lisp, Prolog, and Tcl build each new list of
weights from the old one, and Rust sums
the gradient with iterator chains. awk, BASIC, COBOL, Go, C, C++, Java, Pascal, Lua, and Ada add it up
in an explicit loop, and PostgreSQL, which has no loops at all, makes each step
one row of a `WITH RECURSIVE` query.

Watch, too, that every weight moves from the same gradient. Go's notes show
what happens if you update inside the loop over students: the intercept
comes out 0.758788 instead of 0.753594. And watch COBOL, which does all of
this in fixed-point decimals. With 18 decimal places it matches the
floating-point languages to 14, but its 150,000 calls to `EXP` make it the
slowest version here.
