You grow 8 tomato plants with compost and 8 without, and the compost plants
yield more. But plants vary, so some gap would show up even if compost did
nothing. A hypothesis test asks how surprising your gap would be if chance
were the only thing at work.

## The dataset

`data.csv` holds the yield of 16 plants in whole grams, with a `group`
column of `control` or `compost`. The data is synthetic, and we know the
truth: `make_data.py` gives compost plants an extra 50 g on average. The
control plants average 403.875 g and the compost plants 453.625 g, a gap
of 49.75 g.

## The question, turned around

The test starts by assuming the dull explanation, called the **null
hypothesis**: compost makes no difference. If that's true, the labels are
arbitrary. Each plant would have yielded the same whatever group it was
in, so any 8 of the 16 could just as well have been the compost group.

That gives us a way to see what chance alone produces. Choose 8 plants,
call them compost, and compute the gap. There are exactly

$$
\binom{16}{8} = 12{,}870
$$

ways to choose them, few enough to try every one. That's what makes this
an **exact permutation test**: no random shuffling and no table of a
probability distribution, just counting.

## The method

1. **Observe** the real gap: compost total minus control total.
2. **Relabel** every possible way, 12,870 in all, and compute each gap the
   same way.
3. **Count** the relabelings whose gap is at least as large as the real
   one, in either direction. Counting both directions makes the test
   **two-sided**: we'd be just as surprised if compost cut the yield.
4. **Divide** by 12,870. That fraction is the **p-value**.

Both groups have 8 plants, so comparing totals is the same as comparing
means. Totals are whole numbers, though, so every language compares them
exactly. That matters: 6 relabelings give a gap of exactly 49.75 g, and
they must all count as "at least as large".

## Reading the result

398 of the 12,870 relabelings give a gap of 49.75 g or more, so

$$
p = \frac{398}{12{,}870} \approx 0.031
$$

If compost did nothing, a gap this large would turn up about 3% of the
time. By the common convention of rejecting the null hypothesis when $p$
is below 0.05, we'd conclude compost helps.

Two cautions. First, $p$ is not the chance that compost does nothing. It's
the chance of a gap this large *if* compost does nothing.

Second, small samples swing. We drew 20 datasets from the same recipe,
each with the same true 50 g effect, and chose this one as a clear
example. Their p-values ran from 0.0002 to 0.77, and only 8 of the 20 fell
below 0.05. A large p-value means the data can't rule chance out, not that
the effect is absent. With 8 plants a group, a real 50 g effect is easy to
miss.

## What to watch for across languages

None of these implementations calls a ready-made test. SciPy's
`permutation_test`, R's coin package, and Apache Commons Math have them,
but they aren't installed. The interest is in how each language lists the
12,870 splits.

Some hand you the list. Python's `itertools.combinations`, Ruby's
`combination`, R's `combn`, and Octave's `nchoosek` give every choice of 8
plants in one call. Haskell's `subsequences` and Prolog's `subseq` come
close, but they list all 65,536 subsets, and you still have to keep the
ones of size 8.

Others count in binary. Each number from 0 to 65,535 is a 16-bit mask of
which plants are chosen, and you keep the masks with exactly eight 1s:
Java's `Integer.bitCount`, Julia's `count_ones`, and Common Lisp's
`logcount` count them for you. C++ goes the other way, with
`std::next_permutation` stepping through the arrangements of eight 0s and
eight 1s. APL builds the whole 16 by 65,536 table of masks at once.

The rest write the recursion out, taking or skipping one plant at a time,
and that's where the traps are. In awk, a function's only local variables
are extra parameters. Leave the loop counter out of the list, and every
call shares one counter: the program reports 9 splits instead of 12,870.
In Go, a closure declared with `:=` can't call itself, so it's declared
with `var` first. BASIC, COBOL, and Fortran skip recursion and step from
one split to the next in dictionary order, and bwBASIC adds a trap of its
own: it runs a `FOR` loop's body once even when the loop should run zero
times.

Finally, watch the division that prints the means. Lua's and Elixir's `/`
always give a float. Tcl's rounds two integers down, giving 403, so it
converts with `double()` first, and Common Lisp's gives the exact fraction
3231/8, so it divides by a double instead.
