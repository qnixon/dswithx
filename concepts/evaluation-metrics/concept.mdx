Most pages on this site end with an accuracy: the share of predictions a
model gets right. That's a fine first number, but it treats every mistake
alike and hides what the model actually does. Evaluation metrics split the
mistakes apart so you can see them.

## The dataset

`data.csv` holds 200 parts from a production line. `defective` is 1 if the
part really is defective and 0 if it's good, and `score` is what a model
said, from 0 to 1, with higher meaning "more likely defective". The data
is synthetic (see `make_data.py`), and 33 of the 200 parts are defective.

A score is not yet a decision. You choose a **threshold** and flag every
part whose score reaches it.

## Four counts

Every part lands in one of four boxes, the **confusion matrix**. At a
threshold of 0.5:

| | Flagged | Not flagged |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Defective | 15 true positives | 18 false negatives |
| Good | 6 false positives | 161 true negatives |

Every metric on this page is a ratio of those counts:

$$
\text{precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}
\qquad
\text{recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}
$$

**Precision** asks: of the parts we flagged, how many were defective?
Here, 15 of 21, or 0.71. **Recall** asks: of the defective parts, how many
did we flag? Here, 15 of 33, or 0.45. **F1** combines the two into one
number, their harmonic mean, which is 0.56 here and stays low unless both
are high.

## Why accuracy misleads

At 0.5, the model is right about 176 of 200 parts, an accuracy of 0.88.
That sounds good until you try a "model" that never flags anything. It's
right about all 167 good parts, an accuracy of 0.835, and it catches no
defects at all. When one class is rare, accuracy mostly measures how
common the other class is.

## Moving the threshold

Suppose a missed defect costs far more than a second look at a good part.
Lower the threshold to 0.3:

| Threshold | 0.5 | 0.3 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Defects caught (TP) | 15 | 28 |
| Defects missed (FN) | 18 | 5 |
| False alarms (FP) | 6 | 29 |
| Precision | 0.71 | 0.49 |
| Recall | 0.45 | 0.85 |
| F1 | 0.56 | 0.62 |
| Accuracy | 0.880 | 0.830 |

Now the model catches 28 of the 33 defects, at the price of 29 false
alarms. F1 rises from 0.56 to 0.62. But accuracy *falls*, to 0.83, below
the model that does nothing. By accuracy alone, you'd keep the threshold
that misses more than half the defects. Which threshold is right depends
on what each kind of mistake costs, and no single number can decide that
for you.

## One number for every threshold

The **AUC**, the area under the ROC curve, measures the scores themselves,
before any threshold. It's the chance that a randomly chosen defective
part scores higher than a randomly chosen good one. We can count it
directly: compare all 33 × 167 = 5,511 pairs, score a win as 1 and a tie
as ½, and divide by 5,511. Here the AUC is 0.901. A model that guessed at
random would get 0.5, and a perfect ranking 1.

Three pairs tie exactly, so ties matter. Every implementation scores a win
as 2 points and a tie as 1, which keeps the total a whole number until the
final division by 2 × 5,511.

## What to watch for across languages

None of these implementations calls a ready-made metric. scikit-learn's
`roc_auc_score`, R's pROC package, and Perl's Statistics::ROC compute
these, but they aren't installed. The work is counting and dividing, and
both have traps.

Dividing one count by another is the first. In C and PostgreSQL, `7/2` is
3, so a ratio of counts needs a conversion before the division. Go, Swift,
OCaml, and Ada won't divide an integer by a float at all until you convert
one of them. Common Lisp goes the other way and keeps exact fractions:
precision at 0.5 is `5/7`, and the AUC is exactly `9931/11022` until it's
printed.

Ties in the AUC are the second, because they depend on what "equal"
means. In Java, `==` on two boxed `Double` objects compares the objects,
not the values, so with boxed loop variables the 3 tied pairs would
quietly count as losses. APL's `=` allows a tiny tolerance, so
`0.3=0.1+0.2` is true there, which is harmless here because distinct
scores differ by at least 0.001. awk, COBOL, and Common Lisp sidestep the
question by reading each score as a whole number of thousandths.

Finally, watch how each language sorts a part into one of the four boxes.
Rust and Swift match on the pair (flagged, defective) and refuse to compile
if a case is missing. Perl and PHP score each AUC pair in one step:
`<=>` gives 1, 0, or −1, and adding 1 makes that the 2, 1, or 0 points
for a win, a tie, or a loss. Rust and Elixir can't even call the
false-negative count `fn`, since that's a keyword in both.
